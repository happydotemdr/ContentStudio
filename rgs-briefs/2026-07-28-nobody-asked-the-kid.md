# Grounding Brief: nobody asked the kid

## Pairing
- **Thinker:** Charlotte Mason, *Home Education* — play "is, in its turn, fully as important as
  lessons," and "the happiness of the child is the condition of his progress"; the same book
  holds that children "narrate by nature" and that this account of their own experience "is
  allowed to lie fallow" because "so ingrained is our contempt for children that we see nothing
  in this but Bobbie's foolish childish way"
  [THINKER: Charlotte Mason, Home Education, quote-ok]
- **Research:** Visek et al. 2015 (R10 — Fun Integration Theory / FUN MAPS, *Journal of Physical
  Activity and Health* 12(3):424–433) — 81 fun-determinants grouped into 11 fun-factors, with
  **Trying Hard, Positive Team Dynamics and Positive Coaching** on top; **winning is not one of
  the 11 factors at all** and sits roughly 40th among the 81 determinants
  [RESEARCH: Visek et al. 2015, Quality Medium–High / Scope Moderate / Depth Deep]
- **Why this pairing:** Mason states plainly that happiness is "the condition of…progress" and
  ranks vigorous play as equal in importance to formal instruction. Visek's research
  operationalizes this: fun is a measurable, designable construct dominated by effort, team
  dynamics and coaching quality, with winning ranking low — and a coach who maximizes fun isn't
  sacrificing development but enabling it. An interpretive echo drawn by this brand, not a claim
  Visek's team cites Mason.

## Reframe
Argumentative body: the gap between the two scoreboards is not a verdict on the parent's choices
and not evidence the season was wasted. It is a piece of information the whole system is built
not to collect — standings, rankings and exposure events all record the adult scoreboard, and
nothing in the machine has a field for the child's. Mason's claim is that the child's side is not
the softer measure but the load-bearing one: "the happiness of the child is the condition of his
progress," and play "is, in its turn, fully as important as lessons, as regards both bodily
health and brain-power." The kid isn't keeping a consolation scoreboard. They're keeping the one
that decides whether they're still playing next year.
[THINKER: Charlotte Mason, Home Education, quote-ok]

The relief beat: the villain is a system with no field for the child's answer — never the parent,
who was handed a standings sheet and told it was the report card. And the action is small,
free, and available this week: ask one question on the drive home, and let the answer stand.

Kicker line for the Loop/CTA echo: **"You don't have to change anything to find out. You just
have to ask — and believe the answer."**

**Quotability at this beat: quote-ok** — both Mason lines may appear on-screen verbatim.

**Narrowing applied to the Short's claims** (carried from
`rgs-briefs/2026-07-28-rgs-debut-sparks.md`, "Gaps and flags"): R10 establishes **what kids
report makes sport fun**, and that winning is not near the top of that list. It does **not**
establish what any individual child will say, that a specific season was good or bad, or that
asking the question changes an outcome. Publishable form: *"researchers asked hundreds of kids
what makes sport fun — winning wasn't in the top tier."* Unpublishable forms: *"your kid doesn't
care about winning"* (a claim about one child from group data), and *"ask this question and your
kid will stay in sport"* (an intervention claim R10 does not support).
