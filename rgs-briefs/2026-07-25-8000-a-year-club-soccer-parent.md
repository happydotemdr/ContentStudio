- **Why this pairing:** Adler argues that "ambition," "drive," and "go-getting" are the
  respectable words people substitute for plain vanity — a socially acceptable story that hides
  the real motive even from the person telling it. S5 documents a ~$40B pay-to-play youth-sport
  economy substantially driven by the scholarship dream, with some families spending up to
  ~10.5% of gross income chasing an outcome that rarely arrives. The parallel: the club-soccer
  parent who "can't say why" isn't lying — Adler's mechanism is that the honest answer (status,
  vanity, keeping pace) has already been relabeled as something nobler ("ambition for my kid")
  before it ever reaches consciousness. This is an editorial lens on the psychology of the
  spending, not a claim S5's sources measured vanity as a construct or that Post et al. cite
  Adler.

## Payoff
Use S5's own numbers rather than re-deriving one: the youth sports market broadly exceeds $40B,
average kids now play fewer sports than in 2019 as the system narrows around fewer, more
specialized paths, and the scholarship dream driving much of the spend rarely pays off (Post,
Rosenthal & Rauh 2019, *Sports* 7(12):247 — youth baseball parents spend $3,000–7,000/yr, some up
to ~10.5% of gross income, chasing long-odds scholarship outcomes). Per S5's own cautionNote,
keep the $40B/market-structure framing labeled as reporting-grade and anchor the hard claim on
the peer-reviewed Post et al. figure — don't present the journalistic $40B number with the same
evidentiary weight as the survey finding.

## Reframe
It was never dishonesty — that's the good news. Adler's insight isn't "you're lying to yourself,"
it's that the story ("ambition," "her future," "keeping the door open") is what vanity always
sounds like from the inside, in anyone, not a personal failing unique to this parent. Naming the
mechanism is what makes it optional: you can keep playing club soccer without the unexamined
comparison instinct steering the checkbook.
